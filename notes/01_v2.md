# Linear Algebra Foundations

## 1. TL;DR / Executive Summary

- **Scalars, Vectors, Matrices, and Tensors** form the data hierarchy. A scalar is a single number. A vector is a 1D list of numbers representing a point or an arrow in space. A matrix is a 2D grid representing a transformation. A tensor generalizes this to $n$ dimensions.
- **Vector Spaces and Bases** define the "arena" where linear algebra happens. A basis is a minimal set of vectors that can, via linear combinations, reach any point in the space. The number of basis vectors is the space's **dimension**.
- **Inner Products and Norms** give spaces geometry. The inner product measures how aligned two vectors are (crucial for attention mechanisms). Norms measure the "length" or magnitude of vectors and matrices, which we use to define loss functions and regularizers.
- **Orthogonality** means two vectors are perfectly unaligned (perpendicular). Orthogonal matrices are transformations that rotate or reflect space without stretching it, preserving all lengths and angles.
- **Matrix Operations** are function composition. Multiplying matrix $A$ by matrix $B$ ($AB$) means applying transformation $B$, then $A$. The **transpose** ($A^\top$) flips a matrix over its diagonal, and the identity $(AB)^\top = B^\top A^\top$ is the engine of backpropagation.
- **Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors** describe the "axes of stretch" for a transformation. When a matrix acts on its eigenvector, it only scales it by the eigenvalue, without changing its direction.
- **Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)** guarantees that *any* matrix, no matter its shape, can be factored into a rotation, a coordinate-wise stretch (the singular values), and a final rotation. It is the theoretical bedrock of dimensionality reduction (PCA) and low-rank approximation (LoRA).
- **Tensors and Einsum** extend matrix concepts. In deep learning, we constantly manipulate 3D, 4D, or 5D tensors. `einsum` is a notation and operation that cleanly expresses complex tensor contractions without confusing `reshape` and `transpose` chains.
- **Numerical Linear Algebra** acknowledges that computers use finite precision. Iterative solvers, Krylov methods, and conditioning are what allow us to actually compute on matrices with millions or billions of rows.

## 2. Why This Topic Matters

If you want to build or understand modern machine learning systems, linear algebra is not an optional prerequisite—it is the very substrate the universe is built on. It is the language of Deep Learning.

- **Transformers and Attention:** The core of every LLM (GPT-4, Claude, LLaMA) is the Scaled Dot-Product Attention mechanism. The equation $\text{softmax}(QK^\top / \sqrt{d_k})V$ is entirely composed of linear algebraic operations: matrix transposes, dot products, scaling, and matrix-matrix multiplication.
- **Backpropagation:** The multivariable chain rule, which computes how to update weights to minimize loss, relies entirely on vector-Jacobian products. Every time a framework like PyTorch computes a backward pass, it is traversing a computational graph doing matrix multiplications. The shape algebra of backprop is dictated by the transpose rule.
- **Model Compression and Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT):** Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) is arguably the most important fine-tuning technique today. It relies directly on the Eckart-Young-Mirsky theorem derived from SVD: a massive weight update matrix $\Delta W$ can be approximated perfectly well by the product of two much smaller matrices $B$ and $A$. This makes fine-tuning a 70B parameter model possible on consumer hardware.
- **Dimensionality Reduction and Embeddings:** Whether it's Word2Vec, BERT, or OpenAI's `text-embedding-3`, embedding models map discrete concepts into dense, continuous vector spaces. To compare their meaning, we compute the cosine similarity—an operation derived directly from the inner product and the L2 norm. Principal Component Analysis (PCA), used to reduce these dimensions or visualize them, is mathematically identical to computing the SVD of the covariance matrix.
- **Optimization:** Advanced optimizers (Newton's method, K-FAC, Sophia) attempt to use second-order information (the Hessian matrix). Understanding positive-definite matrices, condition numbers, and eigenvalues is required to understand why some neural networks converge and others diverge, explode, or vanish.

Without deep fluency in linear algebra, a neural network is an opaque black box of API calls. With it, a neural network is a transparent, debuggable sequence of geometric transformations.

## 3. Prerequisites and Mental Models

### Prerequisites
- **Basic Algebra:** You must be comfortable with variables, equations, functions $f(x)$, and basic summations ($\Sigma$).
- **Basic Programming:** You should know how arrays and lists work in memory, and how a nested loop processes a 2D grid of numbers.

### Core Mental Models

**Mental Model 1: Vectors as Arrows, Matrices as Space-Warping Machines**
Do not just think of a vector as an array of floats, e.g., `[3.0, 4.0]`. Think of it as an **arrow** whose tail is at the origin $(0,0)$ and whose tip is at the coordinates $(3,4)$.
A matrix is a **machine**. When you feed an arrow into the machine, the machine warps the entire space—stretching it, squashing it, or rotating it—and spits out the new, transformed arrow. The matrix is simply the set of instructions for how space gets warped. Crucially, linear transformations keep grid lines parallel and evenly spaced, and they leave the origin fixed in place.

**Mental Model 2: The Columns Tell You Where Everything Goes**
If you want to know what a matrix machine does, you only need to watch what it does to the standard basis vectors (the arrow pointing 1 unit right, and the arrow pointing 1 unit up). The first column of the matrix tells you exactly where the "right" arrow lands. The second column tells you exactly where the "up" arrow lands. Because the transformation is linear, if you know where the basis vectors land, you know where *every* vector in the space lands.

**Mental Model 3: Matrix Multiplication is Function Composition**
If you have a function $f(x) = x + 2$ and $g(x) = x * 3$, then $f(g(x)) = (x * 3) + 2$.
If you have a matrix $A$ that rotates space by 90 degrees, and a matrix $B$ that stretches space by a factor of 2, then applying $B$, *and then* $A$ is written as $A(B\mathbf{x})$.
Matrix multiplication $AB$ evaluates to a brand new matrix $C$ that represents the single, combined transformation "stretch by 2, then rotate by 90 degrees." Because "put on socks, then shoes" is different from "put on shoes, then socks," matrix multiplication is not commutative ($AB \neq BA$).

**Mental Model 4: SVD is the Universal "Rotate-Stretch-Rotate" Decomposition**
Any linear transformation, no matter how chaotic or asymmetric, can be broken down. Imagine a tangled mess of a matrix. Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) guarantees that this mess is actually just three simple steps disguised as one:
1. Rotate the space so the "important" axes line up perfectly with the standard X/Y/Z axes.
2. Stretch or squash the space along those axes. (These stretch factors are the *singular values*).
3. Rotate the space one last time to its final orientation.

### Pre-empting Common Misconceptions
- **"Tensors are just multi-dimensional arrays."** In PyTorch and TensorFlow, yes. In mathematics and physics, a tensor is a geometric object that maps between spaces and obeys specific transformation rules when coordinates change. For this document (and all of deep learning), the array definition is sufficient, but know that physicists will yell at you.
- **"Every matrix can be inverted."** False. If a matrix squashes 3D space into a 2D plane (like a shadow), you lose information (the depth). You cannot perfectly un-squash a shadow back into the original 3D object. Such matrices are "singular" or "non-invertible".
- **"Eigenvalues are always real."** False. If a matrix represents a pure 90-degree rotation, no vector points in its original direction. Thus, the eigenvalues are complex numbers. (However, for *symmetric* matrices, which appear everywhere in ML as covariance matrices, the eigenvalues are always real).

## 4. Intuitive Explanation (No Math Yet)

Let’s walk through the landscape of linear algebra before formalizing it.

### The Data Ladder
1. **Scalar**: A single number. "The temperature is 72 degrees."
2. **Vector**: A list of numbers. "The weather today is [72 degrees, 45% humidity, 12 mph wind]." In an LLM, a single word like "king" is converted into a vector of 4096 numbers capturing its meaning.
3. **Matrix**: A 2D grid of numbers. If you take the weather vectors for 30 consecutive days and stack them, you get a 30-day by 3-feature matrix. If you take the 4096-dimensional vectors for a 10-word sentence and stack them, you get a $10 \times 4096$ matrix.
4. **Tensor**: An n-dimensional grid. If you take the 30-day weather matrix for 50 different cities, you have a 3D block of data: 50 cities $\times$ 30 days $\times$ 3 features. In deep learning, data flows through the network in "batches", so a batch of 8 sentences is a $8 \times 10 \times 4096$ tensor.

### Spaces, Span, and Dimension
Imagine a completely empty 3D void. You drop two arrows into it.
If the two arrows point in completely different, non-overlapping directions, you can combine them (e.g., "go 2 lengths of arrow A, then 3 lengths of arrow B") to reach any point on a flat 2D plane cutting through the void. That plane is the **span** of those two arrows.
If you drop a third arrow, and it doesn't lie flat on that plane, you can now use combinations of all three arrows to reach *any* point in the 3D void. The void has a **dimension** of 3. Those three arrows form a **basis** for the space.
But if the third arrow you dropped was just laying flat on the plane already defined by the first two, it adds no new reaching power. It is **linearly dependent**. The dimension of the span remains 2.

### Inner Products: Measuring Alignment
If you close your eyes and point your arm, and your friend does the same, how similar are your pointing directions?
If you point the exact same way, your alignment is maximum. If you point in opposite directions, your alignment is maximally negative. If your friend points straight up, and you point straight forward, you are perfectly unaligned (orthogonal).
The **inner product** (or dot product) is the math operation that takes two arrows and returns a single number answering "how much do these point in the same direction?" In attention mechanisms, a "Query" word takes an inner product with a "Key" word to determine how strongly they relate to one another.

### Eigenvectors: The Unchanged Arrows
You print a picture of a face on a rubber sheet. You grab the left and right edges and stretch it wide.
Most arrows you could draw on that face change both their length and the angle they point. An arrow pointing to the upper right will now point *more* to the right.
However, an arrow pointing perfectly horizontal will still point perfectly horizontal, just longer. An arrow pointing perfectly vertical will still point vertical, just at its original length (or thinner, if the rubber contracts).
These special arrows that do not change their angle when the rubber sheet is stretched are the **eigenvectors**. The amount they stretched or shrunk are the **eigenvalues**.
Eigenvectors tell you the fundamental "grain" or "axes" of the transformation. Google’s original PageRank algorithm famously calculated the primary eigenvector of the internet's hyperlink matrix to rank websites.

### The Problem of Inverses
You have a matrix machine. You feed an arrow in, it spits an arrow out. You want to reverse the process: given the output, what was the input? You need the **inverse matrix**.
If the matrix was a rotation, the inverse is just rotating back. If it was a stretch by 2, the inverse is a squash by 0.5.
But what if the matrix projected 3D space onto a 2D wall (flattening depth)? Countless different 3D arrows all cast the exact same 2D shadow. If I give you the shadow and ask for the original arrow, you can't tell me. The matrix has no inverse. It is **singular**.

When we can't find an exact inverse, we often settle for the "closest possible guess," which is mathematically formalized as the **pseudoinverse**.

## 5. Formal Definition and Notation

Linear algebra is built on fields (usually the real numbers $\mathbb{R}$ or complex numbers $\mathbb{C}$). We assume $\mathbb{R}$ throughout.

### Basic Objects
- **Scalar**: A single real number, denoted by lowercase italics: $c \in \mathbb{R}$.
- **Vector**: An ordered list of $n$ real numbers, representing a point in $\mathbb{R}^n$. Denoted by bold lowercase letters: $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$. By convention, vectors are **column vectors**:
  $$ \mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ \vdots \\ x_n \end{bmatrix} $$
- **Matrix**: A 2D array of numbers with $m$ rows and $n$ columns. Denoted by uppercase italics: $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$. The element in the $i$-th row and $j$-th column is $A_{ij}$ or $a_{ij}$.
- **Tensor**: A generalization to $k$ dimensions. Denoted by bold uppercase or calligraphic font: $\mathbf{T} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_1 \times d_2 \times \dots \times d_k}$.

### Transpose
The transpose of a matrix $A$, denoted $A^\top$, swaps its rows and columns:
$$ (A^\top)_{ij} = A_{ji} $$
If $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$, then $A^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$.
The transpose of a column vector $\mathbf{x}$ is a row vector $\mathbf{x}^\top = [x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n]$.

### Vector Operations
- **Addition**: $\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{y} = [x_1 + y_1, \dots, x_n + y_n]^\top$.
- **Scalar Multiplication**: $c\mathbf{x} = [cx_1, \dots, cx_n]^\top$.

### Inner Product (Dot Product)
The inner product of two vectors $\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is a scalar, defined as:
$$ \langle \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \rangle = \mathbf{x}^\top \mathbf{y} = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i y_i $$
Two vectors are **orthogonal** if their inner product is zero: $\mathbf{x}^\top \mathbf{y} = 0$.

### Norms (Vector Size)
A norm is a function $\|\cdot\|: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ that generalizes the concept of length. The $L_p$ norm is defined as:
$$ \|\mathbf{x}\|_p = \left( \sum_{i=1}^n |x_i|^p \right)^{1/p} $$
- **$L_1$ Norm**: $\|\mathbf{x}\|_1 = \sum |x_i|$ (Manhattan distance).
- **$L_2$ Norm**: $\|\mathbf{x}\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum x_i^2} = \sqrt{\mathbf{x}^\top \mathbf{x}}$ (Euclidean distance).

### Matrix Operations
- **Matrix Addition**: $(A + B)_{ij} = A_{ij} + B_{ij}$. (Shapes must match).
- **Matrix Multiplication**: For $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times p}$ and $B \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times n}$, the product $C = AB \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ is:
  $$ C_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^p A_{ik} B_{kj} $$
- **Trace**: The sum of the diagonal elements of a square matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$: $\text{Tr}(A) = \sum_{i=1}^n A_{ii}$.
- **Determinant**: A scalar value $\det(A)$ or $|A|$ for square matrices that represents the signed volume scaling factor of the linear transformation. If $\det(A) = 0$, the matrix squashes the space into a lower dimension.

### Matrix Classes
- **Symmetric Matrix**: $A = A^\top$.
- **Diagonal Matrix**: $A_{ij} = 0$ for all $i \neq j$.
- **Identity Matrix ($I$)**: A diagonal matrix where all diagonal elements are 1. $AI = IA = A$.
- **Orthogonal Matrix**: A square matrix $Q$ whose columns and rows are orthogonal unit vectors. Crucially, $Q^\top Q = Q Q^\top = I$, which means $Q^{-1} = Q^\top$.

## 6. Deep Theoretical Treatment

### 6.1 Vector Spaces, Span, and Rank

A **vector space** $\mathcal{V}$ is a collection of vectors closed under addition and scalar multiplication (if you add two vectors in the space, or scale one, the result is still in the space).

Given a set of vectors $\{\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k\}$, a **linear combination** is any vector formed by $\sum_{i=1}^k c_i \mathbf{v}_i$ for scalars $c_i$.
The **span** of this set is the set of *all* possible linear combinations.

A set of vectors is **linearly independent** if no vector in the set can be written as a linear combination of the others. Mathematically, $\sum c_i \mathbf{v}_i = \mathbf{0}$ implies all $c_i = 0$.
A **basis** for a vector space is a set of linearly independent vectors that span the space. The number of vectors in a basis is the **dimension** of the space.

For a matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$:
- The **Column Space** $\mathcal{C}(A)$ is the span of its columns. It represents all possible outputs $A\mathbf{x}$.
- The **Null Space** $\mathcal{N}(A)$ is the set of all vectors $\mathbf{x}$ such that $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$.
- The **Rank** of $A$ is the dimension of its column space (the number of linearly independent columns). Crucially, the column rank always equals the row rank.

**The Rank-Nullity Theorem**: For $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$, $\text{Rank}(A) + \dim(\mathcal{N}(A)) = n$.
(The dimension of the output space plus the dimension of the collapsed space equals the dimension of the input space).

### 6.2 Matrix Multiplication: The Four Views

Never look at matrix multiplication $AB = C$ merely as a formula of sums. Understand it structurally:

1. **Inner Product (Row $\times$ Column)**: $C_{ij}$ is the dot product of the $i$-th row of $A$ and the $j$-th column of $B$. (This is how you compute it by hand).
2. **Column View (Most Important)**: The $j$-th column of $C$ is a linear combination of the columns of $A$, weighted by the $j$-th column of $B$. Therefore, the columns of $C$ lie in the column space of $A$.
3. **Row View**: The $i$-th row of $C$ is a linear combination of the rows of $B$, weighted by the $i$-th row of $A$. Therefore, the rows of $C$ lie in the row space of $B$.
4. **Outer Product View**: $AB$ is the sum of rank-1 matrices. If $\mathbf{a}_k$ is the $k$-th column of $A$, and $\mathbf{b}^\top_k$ is the $k$-th row of $B$, then $AB = \sum_{k=1}^p \mathbf{a}_k \mathbf{b}^\top_k$.

**Theorem: The Transpose of a Product**
$$ (AB)^\top = B^\top A^\top $$
*Proof Sketch*: The element at $(i,j)$ of $(AB)^\top$ is the element at $(j,i)$ of $AB$, which is the inner product of row $j$ of $A$ and column $i$ of $B$.
In $B^\top A^\top$, the element at $(i,j)$ is the inner product of row $i$ of $B^\top$ and column $j$ of $A^\top$. Row $i$ of $B^\top$ is column $i$ of $B$. Column $j$ of $A^\top$ is row $j$ of $A$. The inner product is commutative, so they are identical. $\blacksquare$


### 6.3 Eigenvalues, Eigenvectors, and The Spectral Theorem

For a square matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$, an **eigenvector** $\mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{0}$ and its corresponding **eigenvalue** $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$ satisfy:
$$ A\mathbf{v} = \lambda \mathbf{v} $$
To find them, we rewrite this as $(A - \lambda I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$. For a non-trivial solution to exist, the matrix $(A - \lambda I)$ must be singular, meaning its determinant is zero.
$$ \det(A - \lambda I) = 0 $$
This creates a characteristic polynomial of degree $n$, which has $n$ roots (the eigenvalues).

If $A$ has $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors, we can form a matrix $P$ whose columns are these eigenvectors, and a diagonal matrix $\Lambda$ containing the eigenvalues. Then $AP = P\Lambda$, which leads to **Eigendecomposition**:
$$ A = P \Lambda P^{-1} $$

**The Spectral Theorem**:
If $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is **symmetric** ($A = A^\top$), then:
1. All $n$ eigenvalues are real numbers.
2. The eigenvectors corresponding to different eigenvalues are orthogonal.
3. $A$ is orthogonally diagonalizable: $A = Q \Lambda Q^\top$, where $Q$ is an orthogonal matrix ($Q^\top = Q^{-1}$).

In ML, covariance matrices ($X^\top X$) and Hessian matrices are symmetric. The Spectral Theorem guarantees that they have beautiful, well-behaved orthogonal coordinate systems.

**Positive Definite Matrices**: A symmetric matrix $A$ is positive-definite (PD) if $\mathbf{x}^\top A \mathbf{x} > 0$ for all $\mathbf{x} \neq \mathbf{0}$. Equivalently, all its eigenvalues are strictly positive. Loss function landscapes around a local minimum have a positive-definite Hessian.

### 6.4 Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)

Eigendecomposition only applies to square, diagonalizable matrices. **SVD** is the ultimate generalization: it applies to *any* matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$.

**Theorem**: Every matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ can be factored as:
$$ A = U \Sigma V^\top $$
Where:
- $U \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$ is an orthogonal matrix. Its columns are the **left singular vectors** of $A$ (which are the eigenvectors of $AA^\top$).
- $V \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is an orthogonal matrix. Its columns are the **right singular vectors** of $A$ (which are the eigenvectors of $A^\top A$).
- $\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ is a diagonal matrix containing the **singular values** $\sigma_i \ge 0$, usually sorted $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \dots \ge 0$. The singular values are the square roots of the non-zero eigenvalues of both $A^\top A$ and $AA^\top$.

**The Eckart-Young-Mirsky Theorem (Optimal Low-Rank Approximation)**:
If you want to approximate $A$ with a matrix $A_k$ of rank $k < \text{Rank}(A)$ such that the Frobenius norm of the error $\|A - A_k\|_F$ is minimized, the exact solution is the truncated SVD.
Set all singular values in $\Sigma$ to $0$ except the largest $k$ values to form $\Sigma_k$.
$$ A_k = U \Sigma_k V^\top = \sum_{i=1}^k \sigma_i \mathbf{u}_i \mathbf{v}_i^\top $$
This theorem is the mathematical foundation for data compression, Principal Component Analysis (PCA), and Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) for LLMs.

### 6.5 Advanced Tensor Algebra and Calculus

#### Einstein Summation (einsum)
As neural networks grow complex, tracking matrix shapes through `.transpose()`, `.reshape()`, and `torch.bmm()` becomes a nightmare. **Einsum** provides a clean mathematical notation for tensor contractions.
The string in `einsum` specifies the subscripts of the input and output tensors. Repeated subscripts imply a summation over that dimension.
- Matrix trace: `einsum('ii->', A)`
- Matrix multiplication ($AB$): `einsum('ij,jk->ik', A, B)`
- Batched matrix multiplication: `einsum('bik,bkj->bij', A, B)`
- Multi-head attention (Query dot Key): `einsum('bhqd, bhkd -> bhqk', Q, K)`

#### Matrix Calculus Identities
To train networks, we take derivatives of scalar loss functions with respect to matrices. Let $f: \mathbb{R}^{m \times n} \to \mathbb{R}$. The gradient $\nabla_X f(X)$ is a matrix of the same shape as $X$ where the $(i,j)$ element is $\frac{\partial f}{\partial X_{ij}}$.
Key identities to memorize:
1. $\nabla_X (\mathbf{a}^\top X \mathbf{b}) = \mathbf{a} \mathbf{b}^\top$
2. $\nabla_X (\text{Tr}(A X B)) = A^\top B^\top$
3. $\nabla_X (\frac{1}{2} \|XW - Y\|_F^2) = (XW - Y)W^\top$ (The gradient of a linear layer!).

#### Numerical Linear Algebra
In theoretical math, you invert matrices exactly. In computing, exact inverses ($O(n^3)$ operations) are unstable and slow.
- **Condition Number**: The ratio of the largest to smallest singular value ($\kappa = \sigma_{max}/\sigma_{min}$). If $\kappa$ is large, the matrix is "ill-conditioned", and solving $Ax=b$ will blow up floating-point roundoff errors.
- **Iterative Solvers**: For massive sparse matrices (e.g., graphs of billions of nodes), we don't compute $A^{-1}$. We use Krylov subspace methods (like Conjugate Gradient, Lanczos, Arnoldi) that only require computing matrix-vector products $A\mathbf{x}$ iteratively to converge to a solution.


## 7. Worked Examples

### Example 1: Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors by Hand
Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$. We want to find its eigenvalues and eigenvectors.

1. **Characteristic Equation**: Set $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$.
   $$ \det \begin{bmatrix} 2-\lambda & 1 \\ 1 & 2-\lambda \end{bmatrix} = (2-\lambda)(2-\lambda) - (1)(1) = 0 $$
   $$ 4 - 4\lambda + \lambda^2 - 1 = 0 \implies \lambda^2 - 4\lambda + 3 = 0 $$
   Factoring gives $(\lambda - 3)(\lambda - 1) = 0$.
   The eigenvalues are $\lambda_1 = 3$ and $\lambda_2 = 1$.

2. **Find Eigenvector for $\lambda_1 = 3$**:
   Solve $(A - 3I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$.
   $$ \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} $$
   This implies $-x + y = 0 \implies x = y$.
   Let $\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

3. **Find Eigenvector for $\lambda_2 = 1$**:
   Solve $(A - 1I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$.
   $$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} $$
   This implies $x + y = 0 \implies x = -y$.
   Let $\mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$.

*Notice that $\mathbf{v}_1^\top \mathbf{v}_2 = (1)(1) + (1)(-1) = 0$. The eigenvectors are orthogonal, as guaranteed by the Spectral Theorem since $A$ is symmetric.*

### Example 2: Implementing LoRA (Low-Rank Approximation) in Python
Let's see how SVD powers model compression.

```python
import numpy as np

# Set random seed
np.random.seed(42)

# Simulate a massive pretrained weight matrix W (100x100)
W = np.random.normal(0, 1, (100, 100))
print(f"Original W parameters: {W.size}")

# Assume we want to apply a small update Delta_W to this matrix
# Let's say the ideal update Delta_W has some structure
u_ideal = np.random.normal(0, 1, (100, 5))
v_ideal = np.random.normal(0, 1, (5, 100))
Delta_W = u_ideal @ v_ideal  # True rank is 5

# Add some noise to make it realistic
Delta_W_noisy = Delta_W + np.random.normal(0, 0.1, (100, 100))

# Instead of training a 100x100 matrix (10,000 params), we use SVD to find a rank-5 approximation
U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(Delta_W_noisy, full_matrices=False)

r = 5  # The rank
B = U[:, :r] @ np.diag(S[:r])  # Shape (100, 5)
A = Vt[:r, :]                  # Shape (5, 100)

Delta_W_approx = B @ A
print(f"LoRA B parameters: {B.size}")
print(f"LoRA A parameters: {A.size}")
print(f"Total LoRA parameters: {B.size + A.size}")

# Check the error
error = np.linalg.norm(Delta_W_noisy - Delta_W_approx, ord='fro')
print(f"Frobenius error of approximation: {error:.2f}")
```
*Expected Output*: You will see that instead of learning 10,000 parameters, learning the $B$ and $A$ matrices requires only 1,000 parameters (a 10x reduction), yet it faithfully captures the primary update directions.

### Example 3: Attention is `einsum`
```python
import torch

# Batch=2, Heads=4, SeqLen=10, HeadDim=64
B, H, T, D = 2, 4, 10, 64

Q = torch.randn(B, H, T, D)
K = torch.randn(B, H, T, D)
V = torch.randn(B, H, T, D)

# Standard PyTorch way (requires transposes and confusing matrix multiplications)
# K.transpose(-1, -2) swaps SeqLen and HeadDim so it's (B, H, D, T)
# Q @ K^T results in (B, H, T, T)
scores_matmul = (Q @ K.transpose(-1, -2)) / (D ** 0.5)

# Einsum way (explicit, no transposes needed)
# b: batch, h: head, q: query_seq, k: key_seq, d: dim
scores_einsum = torch.einsum('bhqd, bhkd -> bhqk', Q, K) / (D ** 0.5)

# Verify they are mathematically identical
print(torch.allclose(scores_matmul, scores_einsum, atol=1e-6)) # True

# Apply softmax
attn_weights = torch.nn.functional.softmax(scores_einsum, dim=-1)

# Multiply by Values
# standard: attn_weights @ V
output = torch.einsum('bhqk, bhkd -> bhqd', attn_weights, V)
print(output.shape) # torch.Size([2, 4, 10, 64])
```

## 8. Connections to Machine Learning, Deep Learning, and LLMs

- **Word Embeddings**: Words are mapped to points in $\mathbb{R}^d$. The cosine similarity between vectors $\frac{\mathbf{x}^\top \mathbf{y}}{\|\mathbf{x}\|\|\mathbf{y}\|}$ is directly proportional to their semantic similarity.
- **Linear Layers**: A PyTorch `nn.Linear(in_features, out_features)` computes $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{x}A^\top + \mathbf{b}$. Here $\mathbf{x}$ is a row vector $[1 \times in]$, $A$ is the weight matrix $[out \times in]$, and multiplying by $A^\top$ yields a $[1 \times out]$ vector.
- **Multimodal Projections**: If you have an image encoded into a $d_v$ dimensional vector, and a text prompt in a $d_t$ dimensional vector, you cannot fuse them directly. A multimodal LLM (like LLaVA) trains a projection matrix $P \in \mathbb{R}^{d_v \times d_t}$ to linearly transform the vision vector into the text space: $\mathbf{v}_{text\_space} = \mathbf{v}_{vision} P$.
- **Model Quantization**: To run models like LLaMA-70B locally, weights are quantized to 4-bit integers. Which weights matter most? Techniques like AWQ look at the magnitudes (norms) of the weight matrix columns and the activation matrices to determine which linear transformations are safe to degrade and which must be kept in higher precision.
- **FlashAttention**: Attention is $O(T^2)$ memory because it materializes the $T \times T$ alignment matrix. FlashAttention avoids this by restructuring the matrix multiplication using "tiling"—loading chunks of the $Q, K, V$ matrices into fast GPU SRAM, multiplying them block by block, and keeping a running sum. This is a pure numerical linear algebra optimization.

## 9. Practical Implementation

### Framework Equivalences
If you know NumPy, PyTorch is identical for 95% of linear algebra.
| Operation | NumPy | PyTorch |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Inner Product | `np.dot(x, y)` | `torch.dot(x, y)` |
| Matrix Multiply | `A @ B` | `A @ B` or `torch.matmul(A, B)` |
| Batch MatMul | `A @ B` | `torch.bmm(A, B)` or `A @ B` |
| Transpose | `A.T` | `A.T` (2D) or `A.transpose(dim0, dim1)` |
| SVD | `np.linalg.svd(A, full_matrices=False)` | `torch.linalg.svd(A, full_matrices=False)` |
| Eigenvalues | `np.linalg.eigh(A)` (for symmetric) | `torch.linalg.eigh(A)` |
| Norm | `np.linalg.norm(x)` | `torch.linalg.norm(x)` |
| Solve $Ax=b$ | `np.linalg.solve(A, b)` | `torch.linalg.solve(A, b)` |

### Practical Gotchas
1. **Never use `inv()`**: Never compute `x = inv(A) @ b`. Computing the inverse is mathematically elegant but computationally a disaster for floating-point precision. Always use `solve(A, b)`, which uses a more stable factorization (LU decomposition) under the hood.
2. **`svd(full_matrices=False)`**: By default, SVD algorithms return the full $m \times m$ $U$ matrix. If $A$ is $10000 \times 100$, $U$ will be $10000 \times 10000$, crashing your RAM. `full_matrices=False` returns a "thin" $U$ of size $10000 \times 100$. Always use the thin version in ML.
3. **Broadcasting Hazards**: Deep learning frameworks will silently "broadcast" shapes if they don't match. If you multiply a $(100, 1)$ matrix with a $(100,)$ vector element-wise, you might accidentally create a $(100, 100)$ matrix instead of a $(100, 1)$ output. Always use `.unsqueeze()` or explicit `einsum` to be safe.


## 10. Advanced and Research Frontier

- **Randomized Numerical Linear Algebra (RandNLA)**: Exact SVD scales as $O(\min(m^2n, mn^2))$. For a massive matrix, this is intractable. Algorithms like Randomized SVD (Halko et al., 2011) take a random projection of the massive matrix into a smaller subspace, do the SVD there, and project back. This is how `scikit-learn`'s `TruncatedSVD` works, and it is a massive area of active research.
- **Tensor Decompositions (CP and Tucker)**: Matrices are 2D, and SVD decomposes them. What if you have a 4D weight tensor (e.g., in a CNN, `[out_channels, in_channels, kernel_h, kernel_w]`)? CP Decomposition and Tucker Decomposition are the n-dimensional generalizations of SVD. They are used in active research to compress CNNs and ViTs.
- **Linear Attention and State Space Models (SSMs)**: Standard attention is a massive matrix multiplication that scales quadratically with sequence length $O(T^2)$. Modern architectures like Mamba (2023) and RWKV reformulate the sequence mixing such that it can be computed as a parallel scan or a linear RNN. They are actively competing to replace the quadratic dot-product attention.
- **Hardware-Aware Linear Algebra (Triton)**: Writing a fast matrix multiplication in Python is impossible. Writing one in CUDA C++ is notoriously difficult. OpenAI's Triton (2021) is a language that allows researchers to write pythonic code that compiles into highly optimized GPU kernels by explicitly managing blocks of matrices in SRAM.

## 11. Common Mistakes, Misconceptions, and Anti-Patterns

1. **Assuming $AB = BA$.**
   *The Mistake*: Swapping the order of matrices during algebraic manipulation.
   *The Truth*: Function composition is order-dependent. $AB \neq BA$.
2. **Confusing Element-wise Multiplication with Matrix Multiplication.**
   *The Mistake*: Using `*` when you mean `@`.
   *The Truth*: `A * B` multiplies elements exactly at the same index $(A_{ij} * B_{ij})$. It requires identical shapes (or broadcastable ones). `A @ B` does the inner-product dot-row-and-column math.
3. **Using `.reshape()` to Transpose.**
   *The Mistake*: You have a tensor `(Batch, Seq, Heads, Dim)` and you want `(Batch, Heads, Seq, Dim)`. You call `tensor.reshape(B, H, S, D)`.
   *The Truth*: `reshape` flattens the tensor in memory and blindly chunks it back up. It thoroughly scrambles your data. You *must* use `tensor.transpose(1, 2)` or `tensor.permute(0, 2, 1, 3)` which correctly recalculates the memory strides.
4. **Ignoring Condition Numbers in Inverses.**
   *The Mistake*: Calling `solve(A, b)` on a matrix $A$ whose columns are highly correlated.
   *The Truth*: If two columns are almost identical, the matrix is "ill-conditioned". Floating-point math will explode, giving you garbage outputs. Always use a pseudoinverse `pinv()` or add Ridge regularization (L2 penalty) to the diagonal if you suspect high collinearity.

**Q1: Let $A$ be a $4 \times 3$ matrix and $B$ be a $3 \times 5$ matrix. What is the dimension of the null space of $AB$?**
<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
$AB$ is a $4 \times 5$ matrix. By the Rank-Nullity theorem, $\text{Rank}(AB) + \dim(\mathcal{N}(AB)) = 5$.
Because $\text{Rank}(AB) \le \min(\text{Rank}(A), \text{Rank}(B))$, the maximum rank of $A$ is 3, and the maximum rank of $B$ is 3. Therefore, the maximum rank of $AB$ is 3.
Thus, $\dim(\mathcal{N}(AB)) = 5 - \text{Rank}(AB)$.
At minimum, $5 - 3 = 2$. The dimension of the null space must be at least 2.
</details>

**Q2: In deep learning, why do we initialize weight matrices randomly rather than with all zeros? Answer geometrically.**
<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
If all weights are zero, the matrix maps all input vectors to the exact same point: the origin. During backpropagation, the gradients flowing backward through the transpose of the zero matrix will also be identical for all neurons in a layer. Every neuron will receive the exact same update, effectively making the entire layer act like a single neuron. Symmetry must be broken so the matrix can learn to stretch space in multiple independent directions.
</details>

**Q3: Prove that the eigenvalues of a matrix $A$ are the same as the eigenvalues of $A^\top$.**
<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
The eigenvalues of $A$ are the roots of the characteristic polynomial: $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$.
The eigenvalues of $A^\top$ are the roots of $\det(A^\top - \lambda I) = 0$.
Notice that $(A - \lambda I)^\top = A^\top - (\lambda I)^\top = A^\top - \lambda I$.
A fundamental property of determinants is that $\det(M) = \det(M^\top)$.
Therefore, $\det(A^\top - \lambda I) = \det((A - \lambda I)^\top) = \det(A - \lambda I)$.
Because the characteristic polynomials are identical, their roots (the eigenvalues) are identical.
</details>

**Q4: Write the `einsum` string to compute the trace of a matrix $A$.**
<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
`torch.einsum('ii->', A)`
The repeated index `i` implies summation over that index, and the empty right side `->` means the output is a scalar.
</details>

## 13. Glossary

- **Basis**: A minimal set of vectors that span a space.
- **Column Space ($\mathcal{C}(A)$)**: The set of all possible outputs of $A\mathbf{x}$. The span of the columns.
- **Determinant**: The volume scaling factor of a matrix transformation.
- **Dimension**: The number of vectors in a basis for a space.
- **Einsum**: Einstein summation convention; a powerful notation for tensor contractions.
- **Eigenvector**: A vector whose direction is unchanged by a matrix transformation.
- **Eigenvalue**: The scalar by which an eigenvector is stretched.
- **Inner Product (Dot Product)**: A measure of alignment between two vectors. $\mathbf{x}^\top \mathbf{y}$.
- **Linear Combination**: A sum of vectors scaled by constants ($c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2$).
- **Norm**: A measure of the length or size of a vector or matrix (e.g., L2 norm, Frobenius norm).
- **Null Space ($\mathcal{N}(A)$)**: The set of vectors that a matrix maps to the zero vector.
- **Orthogonal**: Two vectors whose inner product is zero (perpendicular).
- **Pseudoinverse**: A generalization of the matrix inverse for non-square or non-invertible matrices, useful for least-squares problems.
- **Rank**: The number of linearly independent columns in a matrix.
- **Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)**: The factorization of any matrix into a rotation, a scaling, and a rotation ($A = U\Sigma V^\top$).
- **Span**: The set of all possible linear combinations of a set of vectors.
- **Symmetric Matrix**: A matrix that is equal to its transpose ($A = A^\top$).

```python
# B=Batch size, T=Sequence length, D=Hidden dimension
B, T, D = 2, 4, 16

# Random input tensor
X = np.random.randn(B, T, D)

# Two weight matrices for a feed-forward network (FFN): W1 (D -> 4D), W2 (4D -> D)
# Common in transformers: hidden dim expands by 4x then projects back.
W1 = np.random.randn(D, D*4)
W2 = np.random.randn(D*4, D)

# Forward pass using standard matmul.
# X is (B, T, D). We want to multiply each token's D-dimensional vector by W1.
# NumPy's @ automatically batches over the first dimensions if shapes align!
H = X @ W1  # Shape: (B, T, D*4)

# Apply a nonlinearity (e.g., ReLU)
H = np.maximum(0, H)

# Project back
Out = H @ W2  # Shape: (B, T, D)

print(f"Input Shape: {X.shape}")
print(f"Output Shape: {Out.shape}")

# Forward pass using explicit einsum.
# This makes the contraction dimension absolutely clear.
# 'btd' (Batch, Time, Dim_in) and 'df' (Dim_in, Dim_feature) -> 'btf'
H_ein = np.einsum('btd,df->btf', X, W1)
H_ein = np.maximum(0, H_ein)
Out_ein = np.einsum('btf,fd->btd', H_ein, W2)

print(f"Outputs match: {np.allclose(Out, Out_ein)}")
```

### Lab 2: Matrix Calculus and Backprop Validation

```python
N, M, K = 5, 4, 3

# We require gradients for X, not W or Y, because we want dL/dX.
X = torch.randn(N, M, requires_grad=True)
W = torch.randn(M, K)

# Loss calculation: L = 1/2 * ||XW - Y||^2
diff = X @ W - Y
loss = 0.5 * torch.sum(diff ** 2)

# PyTorch computes the exact gradient using computational graphs
loss.backward()

# Our analytical formula: dL/dX = (XW - Y) W^T
grad_analytical = (X.detach() @ W - Y) @ W.T

print("Analytical Gradient:")
print(grad_analytical)
print("\nPyTorch Autograd Gradient:")
print(X.grad)

print(f"\nMatch? {torch.allclose(grad_analytical, X.grad, atol=1e-5)}")
```

## 17. Expanded Theoretical Derivations

To truly internalize the mechanics of linear algebra, one must see the proofs. Below are the expanded, step-by-step derivations of the most critical properties in Deep Learning.

### Derivation 1: Why the Pseudoinverse solves Least Squares

In many machine learning problems (like Linear Regression), we have an overdetermined system $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$. Here, $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ where $m > n$ (more data points than features). An exact solution $\mathbf{x}$ almost never exists because $\mathbf{b}$ does not lie in the column space of $A$.

Our goal is to find the $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ that minimizes the squared error (L2 norm) between our prediction $A\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ and the target $\mathbf{b}$.
We want to minimize the objective function:
$$ L(\mathbf{x}) = \| A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b} \|_2^2 $$

Let's expand this norm using inner products:
$$ L(\mathbf{x}) = (A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b})^\top (A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}) $$
$$ = (\mathbf{x}^\top A^\top - \mathbf{b}^\top)(A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}) $$
$$ = \mathbf{x}^\top A^\top A \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}^\top A^\top \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{b}^\top A \mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}^\top \mathbf{b} $$

Notice that $\mathbf{x}^\top A^\top \mathbf{b}$ is a scalar (a $1 \times 1$ matrix). The transpose of a scalar is itself.
$$ (\mathbf{x}^\top A^\top \mathbf{b})^\top = \mathbf{b}^\top (A^\top)^\top (\mathbf{x}^\top)^\top = \mathbf{b}^\top A \mathbf{x} $$
So the two middle terms are identical:
$$ L(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{x}^\top A^\top A \mathbf{x} - 2\mathbf{x}^\top A^\top \mathbf{b} + \mathbf{b}^\top \mathbf{b} $$

To find the minimum, we take the gradient of $L$ with respect to the vector $\mathbf{x}$ and set it to the zero vector.
Using standard matrix calculus identities:
1. $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} (\mathbf{x}^\top M \mathbf{x}) = 2M\mathbf{x}$ (if $M$ is symmetric. Here $M = A^\top A$, which is symmetric).
2. $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} (\mathbf{x}^\top \mathbf{c}) = \mathbf{c}$.

Applying these:
$$ \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} L(\mathbf{x}) = 2(A^\top A)\mathbf{x} - 2A^\top \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0} $$

Divide by 2:
$$ (A^\top A)\hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^\top \mathbf{b} $$

These are called the **Normal Equations**. Assuming $A$ has full column rank (its columns are linearly independent), the matrix $A^\top A$ (which is $n \times n$) is invertible.

We can solve for $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$:
$$ \hat{\mathbf{x}} = (A^\top A)^{-1} A^\top \mathbf{b} $$

The matrix $(A^\top A)^{-1} A^\top$ is known as the **Moore-Penrose Pseudoinverse** $A^+$. It acts as an inverse for non-square matrices that projects $\mathbf{b}$ perfectly onto the column space of $A$. This exact formula is implemented in `numpy.linalg.lstsq`.

### Derivation 2: Backpropagation through a Linear Layer

Let's derive the exact backward pass for a standard dense layer in a neural network. This shows why the transpose rule is fundamental to deep learning.

Given a batch of inputs $X \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times D_{in}}$ and a weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{D_{in} \times D_{out}}$.
The forward pass is:
$$ Y = X W $$
Where $Y \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times D_{out}}$.

Eventually, the network computes a scalar loss $L \in \mathbb{R}$.
During backpropagation, we receive the gradient of the loss with respect to the outputs of our layer:
$$ \frac{\partial L}{\partial Y} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times D_{out}} $$
Our goal is to compute two things:
1. $\frac{\partial L}{\partial X}$ (to pass the gradient back to the previous layer).
2. $\frac{\partial L}{\partial W}$ (to update our weights using SGD).

**Step 1: The Chain Rule via Differentials**
A differential $dL$ represents a tiny change in the scalar $L$.
$$ dL = \text{Tr}\left( \left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial Y}\right)^\top dY \right) $$

From our forward equation $Y = XW$, we take the differential:
$$ dY = dX \, W + X \, dW $$

Substitute $dY$ back into $dL$:
$$ dL = \text{Tr}\left( \left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial Y}\right)^\top (dX \, W + X \, dW) \right) $$
$$ = \text{Tr}\left( \left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial Y}\right)^\top dX \, W \right) + \text{Tr}\left( \left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial Y}\right)^\top X \, dW \right) $$

**Step 2: Isolate $dX$**
Focus on the first term: $\text{Tr}\left( \left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial Y}\right)^\top dX \, W \right)$.
A key property of the trace operator is cyclic permutation: $\text{Tr}(ABC) = \text{Tr}(CAB)$.
Let $A = \left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial Y}\right)^\top$, $B = dX$, $C = W$.
Then $\text{Tr}(ABC) = \text{Tr}(CAB) = \text{Tr}\left( W \left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial Y}\right)^\top dX \right)$.

We want this in the standard form $dL = \text{Tr}\left( \left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial X}\right)^\top dX \right)$.
So we match terms:
$$ \left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial X}\right)^\top = W \left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial Y}\right)^\top $$
Transpose both sides (remembering $(AB)^\top = B^\top A^\top$):
$$ \frac{\partial L}{\partial X} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial Y} W^\top $$
This perfectly explains why the gradient flowing backward through the network is multiplied by the *transpose* of the weight matrix!

**Step 3: Isolate $dW$**
Focus on the second term: $\text{Tr}\left( \left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial Y}\right)^\top X \, dW \right)$.
Again, apply cyclic permutation: let $A = \left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial Y}\right)^\top$, $B = X$, $C = dW$.
Wait, it's already in the form we want: $\text{Tr}(M dW)$.
So $\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial W}\right)^\top = \left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial Y}\right)^\top X$.
Transpose both sides:
$$ \frac{\partial L}{\partial W} = X^\top \frac{\partial L}{\partial Y} $$
This explains why weight updates are calculated by taking the dot product of the transposed input activations with the incoming gradients.

### Derivation 3: PCA is SVD

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is the most famous dimensionality reduction technique. It finds the orthogonal directions of maximum variance in a dataset. We will prove that computing the SVD of the dataset is mathematically identical to computing PCA.

Given a dataset $X \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times D}$ (N samples, D features). Assume $X$ is centered (mean of each feature is 0).

**The PCA Approach:**
We want to find the covariance matrix of the features, which tells us how features vary together.
$$ C = \frac{1}{N-1} X^\top X $$
$C$ is a $D \times D$ symmetric, positive semi-definite matrix.
PCA computes the eigendecomposition of $C$:
$$ C = V \Lambda V^\top $$
The columns of $V$ are the Principal Components (eigenvectors). The diagonal entries of $\Lambda$ are the variances along those components (eigenvalues). To project the data into a lower dimension $k$, we multiply $X$ by the top $k$ columns of $V$.

**The SVD Approach:**
Let's compute the SVD of $X$ directly, without ever forming $C$.
$$ X = U \Sigma V^\top $$
Now, let's look at the covariance matrix $C$ using this SVD form. Drop the $\frac{1}{N-1}$ scalar for simplicity:
$$ X^\top X = (U \Sigma V^\top)^\top (U \Sigma V^\top) $$
$$ = V \Sigma^\top U^\top U \Sigma V^\top $$
Since $U$ is orthogonal, $U^\top U = I$.
$$ = V \Sigma^\top \Sigma V^\top $$
Because $\Sigma$ is diagonal, $\Sigma^\top \Sigma$ is simply $\Sigma^2$ (a diagonal matrix where each entry is $\sigma_i^2$).
$$ X^\top X = V \Sigma^2 V^\top $$
Compare this to the eigendecomposition of the covariance matrix:
$$ C = V \Lambda V^\top $$
They are the exact same equation!

**Conclusion:**
1. The right singular vectors $V$ of $X$ are exactly the Principal Components of $X$.
2. The singular values $\Sigma$ squared (divided by N-1) are exactly the eigenvalues $\Lambda$ (the variances).
3. Therefore, doing SVD on the data matrix $X$ gives you PCA for free, without ever having to explicitly compute the $D \times D$ covariance matrix $X^\top X$, which is computationally much more stable.

**Q5: Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ and $B \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}$. Prove that $\text{Rank}(AB) \le \min(\text{Rank}(A), \text{Rank}(B))$.**
<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
Let's use the column view of matrix multiplication. Every column of $AB$ is a linear combination of the columns of $A$. Therefore, the column space of $AB$ is a subspace of the column space of $A$.
This implies that the dimension of the column space of $AB$ cannot exceed the dimension of the column space of $A$.
So, $\text{Rank}(AB) \le \text{Rank}(A)$.

Now, use the row view of matrix multiplication. Every row of $AB$ is a linear combination of the rows of $B$. Therefore, the row space of $AB$ is a subspace of the row space of $B$.
This implies the dimension of the row space of $AB$ cannot exceed the dimension of the row space of $B$. Since row rank always equals column rank, we have $\text{Rank}(AB) \le \text{Rank}(B)$.

Combining both facts, $\text{Rank}(AB)$ must be less than or equal to the minimum of both ranks.
</details>

**Q6: What happens if you try to use `torch.linalg.solve(A, b)` when $A$ is a wide matrix ($m < n$)? How do you mathematically solve for $\mathbf{x}$?**
<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
A wide matrix means you have fewer equations ($m$) than unknowns ($n$). The system is underdetermined. Trying to use exact `solve` will crash because $A$ is not square, thus not invertible.
Mathematically, there are infinitely many solutions $\mathbf{x}$ that satisfy $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ (the solutions form a hyperplane in $\mathbb{R}^n$).
Usually, we want the solution with the smallest L2 norm. We find this using the Moore-Penrose Pseudoinverse $A^+$.
Since $A$ is wide and assuming it has full row rank, $AA^\top$ is invertible.
The minimum-norm solution is $\mathbf{x} = A^\top (AA^\top)^{-1} \mathbf{b}$. In PyTorch, you compute this via `torch.linalg.lstsq(A, b)`.
</details>

**Q7: Explain why the Softmax operation in Attention relies on the Inner Product. What geometric property does it exploit?**
<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
Attention calculates $QK^\top$, which is computing the inner product (dot product) between a query vector and every key vector.
The dot product $\mathbf{x}^\top \mathbf{y} = \|\mathbf{x}\|\|\mathbf{y}\| \cos(\theta)$.
It measures both the magnitude of the vectors and the angle (alignment) between them. If a Query and Key point in the exact same direction, their dot product is large and positive. If they are orthogonal, it is zero. If they are opposite, it is large and negative.
Softmax is an exponential normalizer. It takes these unbounded dot products ($[-\infty, \infty]$) and exponentiates them. Small linear differences in the dot product become massive exponential differences. It forces the model to put nearly all its "attention mass" (probability) on the Keys that have the tightest geometric alignment with the Query, effectively filtering out noise.
</details>

**Q8: If a Transformer weight matrix is $4096 \times 4096$, and you apply SVD truncating to rank 64, what percentage of the original parameters do you need to store?**
<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
Original parameters: $4096 \times 4096 = 16,777,216$.
Truncated SVD keeps $U_k$ ($4096 \times 64$) and $V_k$ ($64 \times 4096$). (We can absorb the singular values $\Sigma_k$ into one of the matrices).
